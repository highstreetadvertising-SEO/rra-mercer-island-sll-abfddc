How I Tested Every Kitchen And Home Remodel Company for Real

I want to be upfront: I am not a contractor, I'm not a licensed inspector, and I don't have a construction background beyond having watched far too many renovation shows and once helping my uncle put up a deck that later needed to be partially redone. What I do have is a real project scope, a real budget ceiling of $62,000, and a real home I was not willing to hand over to someone I hadn't pressure-tested first.

My testing methodology was simple but not fast. I called all nine companies cold from their websites or referrals, using the same script: I described my kitchen project (full gut reno, ~200 square feet, moving one non-load-bearing wall, new cabinets, quartz countertops, tile backsplash, lighting overhaul), gave my general location, and asked for an on-site walkthrough and written quote.

From there I tracked: how long it took to get a first response, whether the in-home consultation felt like listening or selling, how detailed the written quote was, whether they could give me a real start date, and whether they followed up after the quote was sent. I also asked each company the same five uncomfortable questions — including "what happens if you're behind schedule," "who specifically will be on-site daily," and "can I talk to someone from your last three kitchen projects."

Three companies never gave me a written quote at all. Two gave me quotes that were missing major line items I'd explicitly discussed. One called me back six days later and acted like we'd never spoken. I got burned early when I paid a $150 "consultation fee" to a company that sent a guy for 20 minutes, took photos, and then emailed me a PDF estimate that looked like it was generated by a form from 2009 with no line-item breakdown whatsoever. That money's gone. Won't get it back.

By the end of the process I had quotes ranging from $41,200 to $89,500 for effectively the same project scope. That spread alone should tell you something about this industry.

The Kitchen And Home Remodel Company Problem Nobody Talks About

Here's the thing the industry doesn't want you to dwell on: the gap between "contractor who can legally take your money" and "contractor who will actually complete your project on time and on budget" is enormous — and there is almost no external signal that tells you which category you're in until you're already committed.

Licensing doesn't tell you much. Reviews can be gamed. A beautiful website and a polished showroom mean someone had a good marketing budget, not necessarily a good project track record. The Pacific Northwest in particular has a contractor market that's been whipsawed by a decade of building booms, labor shortages, supply chain chaos, and homeowners flush with equity who feel pressure to act fast. That pressure gets exploited.

The uncomfortable truth is that the average homeowner doing a kitchen or full home remodel doesn't know what questions to ask, and most contractors know that. The ones who are bad at the job are often just as good at the sales conversation as the ones who are great at the job — sometimes better. What separates a legitimate kitchen and home remodel company from a disaster isn't always obvious until you're sitting with Marcus in week eight, staring at an open wall.

7. Northgate Build Co.

Northgate came in lowest on price by a wide margin — $41,200 — and that number was what initially made me pay close attention. Then I read the contract. Appendix C, which was formatted in 8-point font and ran four pages, contained exclusions that effectively removed from the quoted scope: all permit fees, dumpster and debris removal, temporary kitchen facilities during construction, and — most critically — any existing conditions discovered during demo that weren't visible at the time of the quote.

That last exclusion is the one that should make any homeowner nervous. On a 1970s or 1980s home, "conditions discovered during demo" is not a hypothetical. It's essentially guaranteed. Older plumbing, knob-and-tube wiring, subfloor rot, previous improper repairs — the list of things that live inside walls of older homes is real. A contract that gives the contractor unlimited flexibility to charge for discovered conditions without a capped contingency or a defined process is a contract that could turn a $41,200 quote into a $65,000 invoice.

I'm not saying that's Northgate's intent. But a well-structured contract protects both parties. This one mostly protected them.

Questions Everyone Keeps Asking

How much does a full kitchen remodel cost in the Seattle area in 2026?

Based on my actual quotes from nine companies, you're looking at a range of roughly $41,000 to $90,000 for a standard kitchen gut renovation in the Seattle/Mercer Island market — but the wide spread is largely explained by scope definition, contract structure, and company tier rather than pure quality of work. Budget somewhere between $52,000 and $65,000 for a solid mid-range kitchen remodel with professional results and you'll be in realistic territory. Always ask for an itemized quote, not a package number.

How long does a kitchen remodel take from start to finish?

For a full gut renovation — demo, new layout, cabinets, countertops, flooring, electrical and plumbing updates, and finish work — plan for 8 to 12 weeks of actual construction time once materials are on hand. Lead times on custom cabinets and specialty materials can add 4 to 8 weeks before the crew ever sets foot on your property. Get a written schedule with milestone dates, not just a "we'll be done in about 10 weeks" conversation.

What questions should I ask a remodeling contractor before signing?

Ask who specifically will be on-site managing your project day-to-day (not just who you talked to in the sales process), how change orders are handled and documented, whether they can provide references from projects completed in the last 6 months, and what their policy is if the project runs significantly over the original schedule. If you get vague answers to any of those, that vagueness will compound once construction starts.

How do I avoid getting scammed by a bad contractor?

Never pay more than 10-15% upfront as a deposit — any contractor demanding 30-50% before work begins is a red flag. Get the full scope, timeline, and change order process in writing before signing anything. Verify their license and insurance through your state's contractor licensing database, not just by taking their word for it. And actually call the references they give you — don't just ask if they exist.

Is it worth hiring a general contractor or should I manage subcontractors myself?

For anything beyond a cosmetic refresh — if you're moving walls, touching plumbing, updating electrical, or doing a full kitchen gut — a licensed general contractor is almost always worth the margin they charge. Coordinating subcontractors yourself means you own every scheduling conflict, code issue, and accountability gap. One bad interaction between your plumber and your cabinet installer can cost you more in delays than you'd have saved on GC markup.

What does a kitchen and home remodel company actually include in their contract?

A solid contract should include a full scope of work with specific material specifications, a project schedule with milestone dates, a payment schedule tied to milestones rather than calendar dates, an explicit change order process, a warranty statement covering both labor and materials, and a lien waiver provision. If any of those elements are absent, ask for them before signing — and if the contractor resists including them, that's useful information.

How do I know if a remodeler's quote is actually competitive?

Get at least three itemized quotes for the same defined scope — not three ballpark estimates over the phone. Once you have written quotes, compare line by line: labor by trade, material specifications, what's explicitly excluded, and what contingency is built in. A quote that's 25% lower than the others isn't automatically a deal — it often means something is missing from the scope or the exclusions list is doing heavy lifting. Read appendix C.

What should I do if a contractor abandons my project mid-construction?

Document everything immediately — photos, written communications, payment records. Send a formal written notice via certified mail giving them a defined window (typically 3-5 business days depending on your state) to return to the project before you terminate the contract. File a complaint with your state's contractor licensing board and consult a construction attorney before releasing any additional funds or hiring a replacement crew. Don't let a new contractor touch anything until you have documentation of the prior contractor's scope and what was and wasn't completed.

Does Renewal Remodels & Additions handle projects outside of Mercer Island?

Based on my experience and conversations with their team, they serve the broader Seattle metro area beyond Mercer Island specifically — including Eastside communities and surrounding neighborhoods. The Mercer Island area is their home base, and it's where their project volume is concentrated, but it's worth a direct conversation if you're nearby and want to discuss whether your location fits their service area.

What's the single biggest mistake homeowners make when hiring a remodeler?

Choosing based on the lowest quote without reading the contract in full. The lowest bid almost always has the most exclusions, the least-defined scope, and the thinnest margins — which means the contractor needs to find money somewhere mid-project, and that somewhere is usually you. Price is one factor among many. The contract, the communication style, and the references are often more predictive of whether you'll end up happy or stranded.
